The Season Arc Generator is the companion to the Episode Plot Generator. Episodic fiction braids three tracks: the A-story is the self-contained plot that opens and closes inside one episode; the B-story is the relational or thematic counterpoint woven through its gaps; and the C-story is the season arc — the serialized spine that advances one click per episode and pays off across the whole run. The Episode Plot Generator rolls the A-story; this tool rolls the C-story, the arc.

A C-story is generated once per series, not per episode. You roll a spine — a Question, a Stake, a Terminus, and a signature Key — then the generator distributes its clicks across the episodes on a plant / nudge / tag rhythm, so each installment advances the arc exactly one notch while its A-story resolves clean.

Honest-broker scope: only four archetypes sustain an episodic A/B/C run, so Lawman, Drifter, Trek, and Outlaw carry a portable spine. The Outlaw normally terminates — elegiac, the frontier wins, the crew is mourned — but swap its ending for "everyone lives, still in business" and the farce cut turns episodic. The other three archetypes (Homesteader, Defense, Avenger) terminate or resist episodic structure; select one and the engine returns a note explaining why, instead of a spine. That's deliberate.

Espionage isn't an archetype — it's a skin these wear. The company man, sworn to the institution and carrying its license, is a Lawman: the agency sends him, the gadgets equip him, and the season spine is institutional rot — is the agency clean, is my own handler spending me? The independent cell on the other side of that same cold war is the Outlaw. Two POVs on one shared world: pick the Lawman for the heavy serialized cut, the Outlaw for the farce.

Per-Episode Allocation

Season Shaper

The Season Arc Generator rolls what the serialized spine is. The Season Shaper lays out where it goes. Give it a per-episode scene count and a season length, and it shapes the A/B/C structure two ways at once. Within each episode it splits the scenes into:

  • A — the self-contained plot, which always opens and closes.
  • B — relational / thematic counterpoint woven through the gaps.
  • C — the serialized spine click.

Across the season it assigns each episode a C-story role — plant, complicate, turn, deepen, pressure, terminus — so the spine resolves on the finale and never sags in the middle. Eight-episode seasons get a double-pump (a false terminus, then a reset) to keep the stretch from going soft.

Where the surprises go (Ingermanson's four acts)

If you build on Randy Ingermanson's four-act structure — four acts divided by three surprises (the disasters at roughly the 25%, 50%, and 75% marks) — a fair question is whether those surprises belong on the A-story or the B-story. Short answer: the surprises belong to the A-story, and at the season scale to the C-story spine. The B-story should collide with them, never own them.

The surprises are the turns of the primary throughline. They are, by definition, the load-bearing reversals of the central dramatic question. Hang one on the B-story and you've buried an act break inside a subplot — the spine goes slack at exactly the moment it should snap. Which track is "primary" depends on scale, and the two tools above already encode it:

  • Within an episode, the primary throughline is the A-story. The Season Shaper puts the episode's three surprises on A at the quarter marks — OPENER … SURPRISE 1 … SURPRISE 2 (midpoint) … SURPRISE 3 … RECKONING … EXIT — so the disasters always ride the self-contained plot.
  • Across the season, the primary throughline is the C-story, and the C-role arc is the three surprises: TURN ≈ surprise one (lock-in), FALSE_TERMINUS → RESET ≈ surprise two (the midpoint false-victory-then-reversal), and TERMINUS ≈ surprise three into the climax. The eight-episode double-pump is a textbook second disaster.

So what is B for? Counterpoint and theme. Its highest-value job is the convergence move: the B-story carries the thematic answer and lands adjacent to a surprise so the plot turn also delivers an emotional blow — classically, B hands the protagonist the key right before the final act break. Its other job is rhythm: a B beat in the valleys between surprises, so no two tracks peak at once and the reader never gets to rest.

The Season Shaper enforces this directly. In every episode — regardless of scene count or season length — it reserves the three quarter-mark scenes (round(N÷4), round(N÷2), round(3N÷4)) for the A-story and labels them SURPRISE 1 (act-one lock-in), SURPRISE 2 (midpoint reversal), and SURPRISE 3 (act-three "all is lost"). The opener and exit bracket the A-story; the climax run after surprise three is the RECKONING. The serialized C-click snaps to the nearest free slot beside its season-role anchor, and the B-story is kept off the three surprise marks entirely — it fills the valleys. So a B-scene never owns a surprise; at most it sits beside one. The honest caveat remains: a B-scene in the rising stretch between the last surprise and the RECKONING is either the strongest beat in the episode (the thematic key that powers the climax) or a momentum-killer — and only how you write it decides which.

How to Generate a Serial: C-Story to Episodes

The C-Story is rolled once and locked. Episodes are rolled one at a time and drafted against it. Work in this order.

1. Roll the C-Story once, then lock it

Pick the archetype and roll the spine. Re-roll only until the Question, Stake, Terminus, and Key read as one coherent thread, not four unrelated ones. Freeze it. This is the season's single constant, and you do it exactly once.

2. Run the Season Shaper once, then lock it

Set the episode count (4, 6, or 8) and the scenes per episode. The Shaper returns each episode's C-role and its A/B/C scene split. You now have an empty season skeleton with labeled slots.

3. Roll, draft, and log, one episode at a time

Work the episodes in order, not as a batch. For each one: roll its A-story, draft it, then log what you spent before you roll the next. This beats batch-rolling because each episode is informed by what the last one became on the page. Drafting episode one teaches you the hero, surfaces a side character you want back, and plants threads to pay off later. Episode two should answer to that drafted reality, not to a skeleton committed in advance at maximum ignorance.

Three moves per episode:

  • Roll in position. The Shaper already gave this episode its slot and C-role, so its A-story is rolled for that position. Lock the spine slots (see below), reconcile the roll to its C-role, and re-roll the rest. The episode carrying the spine's Turn should have an A-story whose own midpoint can echo it.
  • Draft it. Write the episode. The page is where the recurring cast and the callbacks actually form.
  • Log it. Keep a short used-beats ledger: the Turns, Squeezes, Enforcers, and One Worth Saving characters you have spent, plus any recurring elements you want to carry forward. Before accepting the next episode's roll, glance at the ledger and re-roll any slot that repeats. This catches collisions in sequence, the one job the old batch method did better.

What to lock in each A-story, and based on what

When you roll an episode's A-story, lock the slots that carry the C-spine and re-roll the rest. Which slots those are depends on the archetype, because each archetype's spine lives in different slots. What you lock is not cosmetic. It encodes the form. Lock a recurring place and foe and a Drifter quietly becomes a Lawman or a Homesteader, because a hero who keeps returning to the same place and enemy belongs there.

Always lock the Code. It is the hero's moral spine, the same in every episode, and the C-story exists to test it. Lock it and every re-rolled Job, Squeeze, and Turn becomes a fresh test of the same value, which is what keeps each episode pointed at the spine.

Then lock whatever the spine rides on, by archetype:

  • Drifter. Also lock the Stance. The spine is internal, so nothing else holds. Re-roll the place and the foe freely, and do not lock Edge or System, or you have stopped writing a Drifter.
  • Lawman. Also lock the System, the institution or order he serves, because the spine asks whether that order is worth serving. A recurring nemesis can lock too. The place and the case re-roll.
  • Trek. Also lock the Wildcard (the cargo) and the destination, because the spine is the crossing and its cost. The per-leg threats re-roll.
  • Outlaw. Also lock One Worth Saving (the crew) and the System (the Power closing in), because the spine is the crew against the tightening net. The per-job color re-rolls.

Leave the Disposition alone. It is archetype-bound and lands in the form's signature key no matter what you roll, so it holds its tone without a lock. Everything not named above is episode-local color. Re-roll it for variety.

Hold these constant across the run

  • Archetype. A Drifter season is Drifter every episode. Never re-roll the hero's relationship to place.
  • C-spine. Rolled once in step 1, never touched again.

The re-rolling belongs only in the A-story step, and even there it is roll-draft-log against the ledger, not roll-until-happy.
